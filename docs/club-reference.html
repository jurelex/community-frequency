The problem we are trying to understand

In rural Andean and rainforest regions of Peru, geographic isolation can leave communities outside conventional communication infrastructure.

Limited cellular coverage, Internet access, power, and difficult terrain can make communication much harder.

For communities already facing economic and geographic disadvantages, lack of connectivity can become another barrier to opportunity, education, emergency assistance, health services, and connection with the outside world.

This is not a generic mesh experiment

Community Frequency is not a project to test mesh networking in the United States and then look for somewhere to use it.

The intended beneficiaries are rural and geographically isolated communities in Peru.

The technical question is specific:

Can inexpensive, low-power mesh networking provide useful communications without requiring access to commercial cellular or Internet networks?

The technology is a means to address a real community problem.

Step Zero — Peru 2026

The first mission has three objectives:

1. Establish the regulatory baseline in Peru

Determine what hardware, frequencies, power levels, antennas, and configurations can legally be used.

2. Learn and test in Lima

Meet the Peruvian mesh community, exchange experience, compare Meshtastic and MeshCore, and conduct initial testing.

3. Test in northern Peru

Travel to a community near the Peru–Ecuador border and evaluate the technology in a real rural environment.

Objective 1 — Establish what is legal

Before field deployment, Community Frequency will research Peruvian telecommunications requirements and seek meetings/interviews with the Ministerio de Transportes y Comunicaciones (MTC).

The goal is to establish a practical baseline for equipment and configurations that can be legally used in Peru.

Local hobbyists already using particular boards demonstrates availability and community interest — it does not by itself establish regulatory approval.

U.S. rules cannot simply be assumed to apply in Peru.

Objective 2 — Lima

Lima will be the first technical learning environment.

We will meet with members of the Peruvian mesh community to exchange experience and understand what is already being done locally.

A key comparison will be Meshtastic vs. MeshCore.

Both approaches will be tested where practical to examine coverage, reliability, multi-hop behavior, configuration, power requirements, and operational usability.

The goal is not to declare a winner. It is to learn which approach may be more appropriate for the intended problem.

Objective 3 — Northern Peru

The project will then move to northern Peru and meet with a community near the Peru–Ecuador border.

This changes the test from a controlled/urban environment to a real community and a very different terrain and infrastructure context.

We will observe:

  • Actual communication needs
  • Terrain and coverage
  • Node placement and elevation
  • Multi-hop performance
  • Reliability
  • Power requirements
  • Ease of use and maintenance
  • Whether the technology provides meaningful value

Initial Equipment Target

This is the equipment we would like to take to Peru — not equipment that is already acquired or approved for operation.

  • 2 × BQ G3 nodes
  • 2 × external antennas for the BQ G3s
  • 4 × Heltec V4
  • 2 × Heltec T114
  • 4 × GPS/GNSS kits for V4/T114
  • 2 × Seeed Studio Solar Pro 2 nodes
  • 6 × Muzi Works 915 MHz antennas
  • 6 × batteries
  • Cables, connectors, mounting and field accessories

Building the equipment ourselves

Where practical, enclosures, mounts, brackets, and other mechanical parts will be 3D printed.

This helps keep the equipment inexpensive and adaptable rather than relying entirely on proprietary enclosures and accessories.

The project will document the hardware and field configurations so that successful designs can be reproduced or improved later.

The objective is not expensive infrastructure. It is practical, affordable equipment that could eventually make sense in the environments we are trying to serve.

What will determine success?

A successful test is not simply one that achieves a long radio range.

We need to learn whether a system can be:

  • Reliable
  • Affordable
  • Low-power
  • Practical to deploy
  • Usable by people who are not radio engineers
  • Maintainable locally
  • Appropriate for the terrain
  • Useful for the community's actual needs

If the technology does not meet those requirements, that is a useful result too.

How an amateur-radio club can help

Technical knowledge

Advice on RF, antennas, propagation, power, Meshtastic, MeshCore, field testing, and experimental design.

Equipment

Loaners, evaluation hardware, antennas, batteries, GPS/GNSS equipment, or other useful field gear.

Connections

People with experience in rural communications, emergency communications, community projects, Indigenous communities, or international field work.

Sponsorship

Help purchasing the equipment needed to conduct Step Zero.

Why we need funding

Community Frequency is an independent project. The initial equipment must be purchased before the field work can happen.

The GoFundMe is intended to help cover equipment and field expenses, including the mesh nodes, antennas, GPS/GNSS hardware, batteries, accessories, and materials for 3D-printed components.

The target equipment list represents what we would like to take into the field. Funding will determine how much of that kit we can actually acquire.

What comes after Step Zero?

Step Zero is about establishing a factual baseline.

We want to return from Peru knowing:

  • What is legally possible
  • What hardware is practical and available
  • What works in Lima
  • What works in the field
  • How Meshtastic and MeshCore compare in these environments
  • What the communities actually need
  • What the real costs and maintenance requirements look like

Only then can we make an informed decision about whether a larger community project makes sense.

Research the need. Test the technology. Let the evidence shape the solution.
